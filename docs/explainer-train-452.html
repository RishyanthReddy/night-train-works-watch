Train 452 leaves Hannover at 04:05 and reaches Brussels South at 10:52.

Twelve booked stops. It runs through Germany, the Netherlands and Belgium before breakfast — three operators, three files, three sets of names for the same places.

We do not know why. The saved files do not say, and we will not guess. What matters is that a planner reading only one of these files would never learn the other exists.

It is not one bad row. The two records disagree about Antwerp too — about how long the train stands still.

At Antwerp the Dutch archive books an arrival of 09:45 and a departure of 10:03 — an 18-minute stop. The Belgian record books 9:43 to 9:47 — a 4-minute stop, and the train in fact left at 9:47:45.

Those are different claims about the same train at the same platform. One of them drives a planner's next decision. Showing only one is how a plan goes wrong quietly.

The three files even spell the station differently — and count time differently.

The Dutch archive says Brussel-Zuid, Antwerpen-Centraal, and tags each stop with a code like nl-rdt-stop:ATW. The Belgian file shouts BRUSSEL-ZUID and ANTWERPEN-CENTRAAL, and carries its own numeric point codes.

And the clocks are not in the same form: the Dutch times carry +02:00; the Belgian times carry no offset at all.

Two of these names are four characters apart and mean the same platform. A tired human matching them by eye is one keystroke from a wrong answer. Nightdesk never guesses a match from spelling — a link exists only after a person confirms it, and the confirmation is recorded.

By hand, or with the desk — the same morning, twice.

BY HAND what these files ask of a reader

  1. Open three files from three operators — Dutch archive, Belgian observations, works lists.
  2. Find the same run in each. Both say 452, but the Dutch file keys the trip as nl-rdt:452 with its own trip ID, and the Belgian file keys the same train as be:452 — there is no shared key to join on.
  3. Match the stations by eye: Brussel-Zuid against BRUSSEL-ZUID, Antwerpen-Centraal against ANTWERPEN-CENTRAAL.
  4. Notice that one file stamps +02:00 on every time and the other stamps nothing.
  5. Reconcile the 25-minute gap and the 18-vs-4-minute dwell — and remember which file you took each number from.
  6. Do it again tomorrow, when the booked time has moved to a new value.

WITH THE DESK what the tool does instead

  1. Hold each file unaltered, tagged with source, licence and capture moment.
  2. Show the two records side by side, each labelled by what kind of time it holds.
  3. Refuse to match names by similarity — a link exists only when a person confirms it, and the confirmation is recorded.
  4. Read the offsets from declared config, never from eyeballing the row.
  5. Keep the disagreement visible instead of resolving it silently.
  6. Export the record with a hash-chained receipt so the same morning can be re-checked later.

To be exact: the left column describes the work these three files force on anyone who reads them together. It is not a claim about how European Sleeper's staff actually work — we have never seen their process, and we will not pretend otherwise. The right column is what our code does today, and every line of it is checkable in the repo.

That is the whole job: hold, show, refuse — then hand over.

Hold every file with its source, licence and capture moment. Show the two records side by side, each labelled by what kind of time it holds. Refuse to merge, average or rank them. Then hand the decision to a person, with a receipt they can check later.

It never says “train 452 will be late”. It says: here are two dated records, this is the row, this is the file, this is who captured it and when, and this dataset never declares itself complete.

On the 2nd of October that means a planner sees the 25-minute disagreement at Brussels and the 14-minute disagreement at Antwerp in one place, with both receipts — instead of finding out about them after the plan has already been sent.
